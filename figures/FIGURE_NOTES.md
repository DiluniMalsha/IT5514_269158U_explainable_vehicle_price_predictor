# Figure notes

What each figure shows, the numbers in it, and the claim in the report it
supports. Every figure is saved twice: `.png` at 200 dpi and `.pdf` as vector.
The report embeds the PDF version, which stays sharp at any zoom and keeps the
text selectable.

**Figures are numbered by their position in the report, not by file name.** The
file names follow the order the figures were written in, which is not the order
they are read in, so the table below is the mapping. The number is deliberately
*not* drawn inside the images: the report caption carries it, and an image with a
number burned into it contradicts its own caption the moment anything is
reordered.

## How to read figures 4 to 9

Two conventions, both easily misread.

**1. The axes are multipliers, not rupees.** The model is fitted on log₁₀(price),
so each term's contribution adds in log space and therefore *multiplies* in
rupees. A vehicle does not lose "Rs 2 million" for being old; it loses a
percentage. The base the multipliers apply to is the model intercept,
**Rs 9,899,668**.

**2. The shape functions are centred, so a positive value does not mean "raises
price".** It means "worth more than the average vehicle in this dataset at this
feature value". The clearest case is in Figure 8: a 2017 Kia Picanto receives
`age_years` = **×1.08**, which looks as though the model believes age raises
price. It does not. The age curve crosses ×1.00 at **about ten years** and the
mean age in the dataset is **9.2 years**, so a nine-year-old vehicle sits
marginally on the better-than-average side of a curve that falls steadily from
×1.5 to ×0.25. Stating this once, early, prevents the sign being read as a bug.

## Captions, and what each figure supports

**Figure 1 — Target distribution before and after the log transform.**
Asking price has skewness 7.54 and kurtosis 95.58 as collected; after a log₁₀
transform these become 0.04 and 1.41. The left-hand panel deliberately keeps the
full Rs 0–600M axis, so the concentration below Rs 20M is visible as the problem
it is.
*Supports:* the decision to train on log price. Without it a squared-error
objective is dominated by the handful of listings above Rs 100M.

**Figure 2 — Listing counts and median price by condition.**
Used 5,383 (60.1%, median Rs 7.80M), Brand New 1,811 (20.2%, Rs 13.70M),
Reconditioned 1,278 (14.3%, Rs 9.10M), Import 482 (5.4%, Rs 16.70M). Two
measures, so two panels rather than one chart with two y-axes.
*Supports:* the framing of the problem. This is not a pure used-car dataset but
four market segments, which is why `condition` is a feature and why pairwise
interactions matter.

**Figure 3 — Price against age and against odometer, log price axis.**
Spearman −0.577 for age and −0.483 for odometer. The orange line is the median
within each bin, not a fitted regression.
*Supports:* the choice of a model that fits non-linear shape functions. Both
relationships are monotonic but visibly curved, with depreciation steepest in the
first few years — a linear term would misfit both ends.

**Figure 4 — Term importance, mean absolute contribution.**
`age_years` 0.195, `engine_cc` 0.127, `brand_grp` 0.071, `make_model_grp` 0.057,
`mileage_km` 0.031. Blue is a single feature, orange a pairwise interaction.
*Supports:* two claims at once. The model is **mostly additive** — the seven
largest terms are all single features, and all 15 pairwise terms together are
worth about 0.005 of R². And `mileage_km` at 0.031 against `age_years` at 0.195
is the **0.883 correlation showing up in the fitted model**: age has absorbed
most of the shared signal. The caveat raised during exploration now has evidence
behind it.

**Figure 5 — Fitted shape functions for the three numeric features, with the
spread across bagged fits.**
Age runs from ×1.56 at one year to ×0.25 at thirty-five. Odometer is nearly flat
(×1.25 to ×0.8 across 300,000 km), consistent with its low importance. Engine
capacity rises steadily, ×0.6 at 600cc to ×4.2 at 4,000cc.
*Supports:* the non-linearity argument for the EBM over a linear model, and the
honesty of the uncertainty band — it widens visibly where the data thin, which is
the figure stating which parts of the curve to trust rather than a separate
"data density" annotation.

*The orange line on the engine panel is the missing-value bin,* **×0.74**, learned
from 159 rows. InterpretML's own visualisations hide this bin and warn that they
do so; here it is drawn explicitly, because `engine_cc` is blank precisely for
electric vehicles — the value was deliberately not imputed, since an electric
vehicle has no engine size to impute. Read it together with `fuel_type` =
Electric at **×1.50** in Figure 6: the two terms describe the same vehicles from
opposite sides. `mileage_km` also has a missing bin, **×0.36**, but it rests on
**4 rows** and is noise; it is reported in the script output and deliberately not
drawn.

**Figure 6 — Categorical effects, as price multipliers.**
`fuel_type` spreads ×0.95 to ×1.50 (Electric highest), `body` ×0.94 to ×1.58
(Convertible and Coupé/Sports highest), `transmission` ×0.84 to ×1.13 (Manual
lowest), and `condition` **×0.99 to ×1.02 — essentially nothing**.
*Supports:* the most interesting single finding in this section. Exploration
showed Brand New at a median of Rs 13.7M against Used at Rs 7.8M, a 1.76× gap,
yet the model gives `condition` almost no main effect. The reason is that **age
already contains it**: at age 0, 81.3% of listings are Brand New; at age 6–12,
97.3% are Used; past 12, 98.8%. A residual effect does survive within a single
age band (medians spread 1.2× to 1.7×), and the model carries it in the
`make_model_grp & condition` interaction rather than the main term. Condition's
error bars are also wide relative to its effect: the uncertainty swamps the
signal, which is the correct conclusion rather than a defect.

This is the **third** correlated-feature case in the dataset, after age↔mileage
at 0.883 and age↔condition here. The report presents them as one theme, not
three separate caveats.

**Figure 7 — Two pairwise interaction surfaces.**
*Supports:* why the model is a GA2M rather than a plain additive model — there is
joint structure the additive part cannot express. The two panels have **different
colour scales** (±0.35 and ±0.13); forcing a shared scale would render the second
panel invisible, so each is read against its own colour bar rather than compared
by eye across panels.

**Figure 8 — One prediction, fully decomposed.**
A 2017 Kia Picanto: base Rs 9.90M, predicted Rs 6.82M, actual asking price
Rs 6.25M. Brand contributes ×0.73, engine capacity ×0.81, odometer ×0.93.
*Supports:* the per-prediction explanation, and it is exactly what the front-end
displays. The example was chosen as the row whose error is **closest to the
median error of its segment**, not an arbitrary one — an earlier draft picked a
Mercedes that was 23% under-predicted, which demonstrates the mechanism equally
well but invites a reader to judge the whole model from a single unlucky case.

**Figure 9 — Three attributions for the same vehicle as Figure 8.**
The EBM read directly, SHAP run against the EBM, and SHAP run against the random
forest, for the same Kia Picanto. The first two track each other closely; the
third does not.
*Supports:* the measured version of the SHAP comparison. Over 200 test listings,
SHAP reproduces the EBM's own attribution to a maximum absolute difference of
**0.0048** for the ten features that appear in no pairwise term, and the
correlation across all 4,000 attributions is **0.9953**. The forest is the
contrast: its attributions rank the features differently, at a Spearman
correlation of only **0.728** — `make_model_grp` is 0.0608 for the EBM against
0.0086 for the forest, and `transmission` the reverse. Two models of
statistically indistinguishable accuracy tell materially different stories about
why, and nothing in the scores adjudicates between them.

**Figure 10 — Spearman correlation between the numeric features and price.**
Age and odometer correlate at **0.883** with each other. Only the lower triangle
is shown; a correlation matrix is symmetric, so a full grid repeats every number.
*Supports:* the most important caveat in the explainability section. Because the
two are so strongly correlated, an additive model's individual shape functions
cannot be read as "the effect of odometer holding age constant" — the dataset
contains almost no old-but-low-mileage or new-but-high-mileage vehicles to
separate them. The report states this before interpreting any shape function.

**Figure 11 — Share of listings whose price is an exact multiple of each round
number.**
73.6% of prices are exact multiples of Rs 50,000, and there are only 1,597
distinct prices across 8,954 listings.
*Supports:* the construct-validity point — the target is a **human-quoted asking
price**, not a market-determined transaction price. Sellers pick round numbers,
which is a property of the thing being predicted and belongs in the problem
framing.

This rounding is *not* a meaningful floor under RMSE, and an earlier draft of the
report claimed it was. Measured, Rs 50,000 of rounding on the median Rs 9.5M
listing is 0.5%, contributing a standard deviation of only 0.0015 in log₁₀ units
against a model RMSE of 0.0922 — under 0.1% of squared error. It bites only at
the very bottom of the price range, where Rs 50,000 is a tenth of the vehicle.

**Figure 12 — Listings by district, top twelve of 25.**
Colombo alone is 60.3% of listings; the top three districts are 81.1%. All 25
districts appear, but Kilinochchi (6 rows), Mullativu (4) and Mannar (1) fall
below ten.
*Supports:* the sampling-bias disclosure. The defensible claim is a
Colombo-market model with thin coverage elsewhere, not a national one.

**Figure 13 — Listings by model year, 2000 onwards.**
Model years 2020–2022 hold 159 listings in total, against 495 for 2018 and 1,599
for 2026. Sri Lanka suspended vehicle imports from 2020 and relaxed the
restriction only around 2025, so the gap is structural.
*Supports:* the limitation that the model's age response across roughly four to
six years is interpolation between two populated regions rather than something
learned from data. More data would not fill it, because those vehicles were never
imported.

## Chart conventions

- One series per chart gets one colour. Orange marks **emphasis** only (the
  import gap, Colombo) and never encodes a second variable.
- No dual-axis charts. Where two measures appear (Figure 2) they get two panels,
  because aligning two y-scales invents a relationship the data do not contain.
- Gridlines are solid hairlines behind the marks; nothing is dashed.
- Labels are selective. No leader line is drawn across the data.
- The palette was checked with a contrast and colour-vision-deficiency validator
  rather than by eye.
- Figure numbers are not drawn inside the images; the report caption carries
  them.

The palette and these conventions live in `code/figstyle.py`, which the figure
scripts and the Streamlit app all import, so a chart in the app and the same
chart in the report are drawn the same way.
